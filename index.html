The error that survives review

Books drift quietly, and then it is a restatement.

A wrong price gets argued about. The dangerous errors are the ones nobody looks for: a capital account that stopped tying to the ledger three periods ago, an allocation that no longer sums to the pool, a high-water mark lost in a redemption. Nobody re-adds a fund by hand every month, so these survive every review and surface at the audit.

So the platform re-proves the arithmetic of every fund it holds, every morning, and a failure opens a break carrying the fund, the period and the difference. The control comes with the book; there is nothing to commission. Today it runs against our own reference books, because we have no clients yet. On day one it runs against yours.

What it proves each morning, per fund and per period:

  • ✓Every capital account still ties to the ledger
  • ✓Allocations still sum to the pool, so no rounding leaks into somebody's balance
  • ✓A closed position leaves no orphaned tax lots behind to distort next period's basis
  • ✓Every high-water mark survived its redemptions, transfers and restatements
  • ✓Ownership is still derived from capital, never asserted beside it

What it does not do: tell you a price was wrong. It proves the book is not lying to itself. Checking your numbers against a custodian's or an administrator's is a different control, and a different section.

Allocation

One number in. Every investor's share out.

A fund earns one number. Splitting it across investors who arrived on different days, hold different classes and pay different fees is the calculation everything else depends on, and the one most often done in a spreadsheet beside the accounting system.

Here it is a run against the ledger, with each investor's capital roll-forward, the profit and loss attributed gross to net, and a rate of return on both bases. The morning sweep re-adds every run: the parts must equal the pool, exactly.

The daily book

Four questions, one ledger.

An income statement, a statement of changes, a trial balance and a cash blotter are four questions put to the same ledger. Nothing is assembled in a spreadsheet afterwards, so the figures on one are derivable from the figures on another.

Every screen in this section is the same fund on the same date: HEF-I at 30 June 2026. Read them together and they reconcile, which is not the same as matching. Net income of $145,284.59 on the income statement becomes $125,969.93 of investor profit and loss on the allocation run above, and the $19,314.66 between them is the management fee, which posts through the fee path rather than as an operating expense. That bridge is the point: a statement you cannot walk to the next one is a number you are taking on trust.

Analysis

The grid is the report.

Every table in the product groups, aggregates and computes in place: capital accounts by class, allocations by series, expenses by whoever bears them. No export, no pivot, no copy that goes stale the moment the book moves.

The part that took real work is the arithmetic under the grouping. A percentage keeps its true denominator however you slice it, a point-in-time balance declines to be summed across periods, and a subtotal that would be nonsense refuses to compute instead of computing it confidently. Most reporting tools will happily average your averages. This one knows which columns those are.

And a saved analysis has an address. The cut a controller builds on screen can be shared with the team, pulled through the API, and read by an agent over MCP, so an analysis is built once and answers everywhere, including places we have not built yet.

A EUR commitment is EUR. The base amount is a dated valuation of it, struck at each event's own date and never quietly retranslated. Missing a rate is a refusal, not a guess.

Private markets

Closed-end mechanics, not a spreadsheet beside the ledger.

Commitments, calls, drawdowns and distributions are ledger events with their own state, and the capital account is derived from them rather than maintained alongside them. Uncalled is whatever the calls have not taken yet.

Reporting

ILPA, in the ledger rather than in a spreadsheet.

Capital call and distribution notices carry ILPA CC&D component codes. Expense lines carry ILPA line-item codes at the point of posting, not reverse-engineered at quarter end.

Every row in a reporting template maps back to the ledger entry that produced it. Where a concept genuinely has no source, the template says so rather than quietly reporting a zero.

AI

A model can suggest a fee term. It cannot post one.

Every platform in this category now says AI. Read the claims and almost all of them are read-side: ask a question, extract a document, normalise a feed. Very little of it goes near the number that ends up in a capital account, and the little that does tends to boast about needing no human.

The rule here is the opposite, and it is a rule. A person confirms every figure that becomes a fund term or a journal entry. What the model read travels with the number afterwards: the clause, the document, the person who accepted it. That is only possible because the thing reading the document and the thing holding the book are the same system.

Terms read from the agreement

Upload the LPA. The fee schedule comes back prefilled tier by tier, each one carrying the clause it was read from, so confirming a term means reading one paragraph rather than the whole document. The clause stays attached after you confirm.

Onboarding from the mess you have

The spreadsheet, the CSV, the PDF the last administrator sent. A model proposes the mapping and shows you what it was unsure about first. Only what you approve is committed, and a partial commit is a normal outcome.

Provenance is the point

An AI-derived number that cannot say where it came from is a guess with good manners. Every number here arrives with its source attached, and keeps it after the person who confirmed it has moved on.

Where this goes

The same pattern extends to every document that defines money: side letters and their MFN terms, manager statements, capital call notices, custodian files. Each one gets read, mapped and put in front of a person with its source attached. We want the pattern to cover more documents. Needing fewer people was never the goal.

1494

Why the name

In 1494, Luca Pacioli published the Summa de arithmetica, codifying the double-entry method Venetian merchants had already used for two centuries. Debits and credits, a ledger that balances, books that can be checked by anyone who knows the rules.

The method has not needed replacing. The software built on top of it still cannot reliably make a general ledger and a set of capital accounts agree. We took the year.
