A wrong number that agrees with itself

Cash reconciles against the custodian, positions against the administrator. Both work by comparison, so both are quiet when a figure is wrong and nothing else in the system holds a second copy of it to disagree.

Controls · roughly 6 minutes

Where comparison controls stop

Most controls in fund accounting are comparisons: cash against the custodian, positions against the administrator, the general ledger against the sub-ledger. They find a great deal. All of them work the same way, which is that they fire when two records of the same thing differ.

A management fee charged on the wrong base has no second record. Nor does an allocation that split a period's profit on a stale ownership percentage, or a high-water mark that was never reduced when an investor redeemed half their holding. In each case the books balance, every statement foots, and the investor's capital account is internally consistent. The money is not missing. It was never charged, or it was charged to the wrong person.

Why this class survives

An audit tests whether the books are consistent, and they are. These errors tend to surface years later, when somebody rebuilds a fee from first principles and finds it does not come out where it was booked.

Doing the arithmetic a second time

A figure with no counterparty to check against has to be checked against itself: derived again by a different route, from different inputs, and compared. We do that to every fund on the platform each morning, before anyone signs in.

Some of what is re-derived, in the vocabulary of the work rather than of software:

  • ✓Every capital account rolls forward. Opening, plus contributions and withdrawals, plus that account's share of profit and loss, equals closing, to the penny, for every account in every period rather than within a tolerance.
  • ✓Ownership sums to one hundred percent exactly, with the rounding residual placed deliberately rather than left wherever the arithmetic dropped it.
  • ✓The fees charged to investors add up to the fee the fund paid, side letters and differing rates included. If the parts do not sum to the pool, one of the two figures is wrong.
  • ✓Every journal entry balances and the trial balance foots, checked daily rather than at close.
  • ✓What a position is carried at agrees with the ledger's carrying value through every subscription, redemption and return of capital, so the two cannot drift apart between valuations.

A failure opens a break on the same worklist as a cash break, carrying the fund, the period, and the two figures that should have agreed.

Every morning, not at close

An error found during the close is found under deadline, by the person with least appetite for it, when the remaining options are poor. The same error found on a Tuesday is an afternoon's work, which is most of the argument for running the checks daily instead of at period end.

They also do not sample. Every fund is re-derived every day, including the ones with no activity, because an absence of activity is itself worth testing: a fund that has quietly stopped receiving data looks the same as a fund having a slow month until the quarter closes.

Knowing whether a check actually ran

A check that never ran reports nothing. A check that ran and passed also reports nothing. On a dashboard the two are indistinguishable, and the more conditional a check is, the more often it is the first one.

A rule that applies only to funds with an incentive fee has nothing to examine in a book that has none. It stays quiet, and so does a rule that ran and found nothing wrong.

The distinction

A check that has never had anything to examine has not passed. Counting it alongside the ones that did overstates what the system has actually demonstrated.

So the morning run reports what was never exercised as well as what failed, naming each check that no fund gave it anything to work on. Some of that is expected, and it says so: a rule about converted funds has nothing to report on a book containing none. The rest is genuinely untested, and it is listed as untested rather than counted as a pass.

Leaving a cell empty

The same rule applies in the reports. Where a figure cannot be computed honestly the cell is empty and carries its reason, rather than a number resting on an assumption nobody stated. A subtotal that would be meaningless is omitted. A percentage that has lost its denominator does not substitute one.

Somebody queries an empty cell. Nobody queries a number that looks reasonable, which is how a wrong one ends up in an investor statement.

What to ask any vendor

  • ✓Which figures are re-derived, and which are stored and read back? A figure computed once and read thereafter has nothing in the system able to disagree with it.
  • ✓How often do the checks run, and across how many funds? At period end on a sample, or daily across all of them.
  • ✓Can you show me a check that did not run? If everything passes, ask how the system tells a pass from a check that had nothing to examine.
  • ✓Where does a failure go? Ask whether it reaches a log or a worklist with an owner.
  • Which columns can be added, and which cannot, where the empty cell comes from
  • High-water marks and the partial redemption, one error of this kind worked through
  • What the platform does

How we handle it

The checks are how the engine is built rather than something added to it. A rule is written as a statement that must hold for every fund, and it runs against every fund from then on: daily, and again after every release. It reports what was never exercised alongside what failed. The most recent run before this note was published covered every fund on the platform and came back clean.
