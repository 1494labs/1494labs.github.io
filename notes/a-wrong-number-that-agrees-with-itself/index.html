A wrong number that agrees with itself

A break gets investigated because something disagrees. The errors that cost real money are the ones where nothing does: the roll-forward foots, the statement ties, the investor's capital account is internally consistent, and the figure is wrong anyway.

Controls · roughly 6 minutes

The errors nobody goes looking for

Most controls in fund accounting are comparisons. Cash against the custodian, positions against the administrator, the general ledger against the sub-ledger. They work, and they find a great deal, and they all share one blind spot: they only fire when two things disagree.

A fee calculated on the wrong base does not disagree with anything. Neither does an allocation that splits a period's profit on a stale ownership percentage, or a high-water mark that was never reduced when an investor redeemed half their holding. In each case the books balance, every statement foots, and the investor's own capital account is internally consistent. The money is not missing. It was never charged, or it was charged to the wrong person, and there is no second copy of the figure anywhere to disagree with the first.

Why this class survives

These errors pass audits, because an audit checks that the books are consistent and they are. They surface years later, usually when somebody reconstructs a fee from first principles and finds it does not come out where it was booked.

The only thing that catches them is doing the arithmetic twice

If a number cannot be checked against a counterparty, it has to be checked against itself: derived a second time, by a different route, and compared. That is what we do to every fund on the platform, every morning, before anyone signs in.

Some of what gets re-proved, in the language of the work rather than the language of software:

  • ✓Every capital account rolls forward. Opening, plus contributions and withdrawals, plus that account's share of profit and loss, equals closing. To the penny, for every account, in every period. Not to a tolerance.
  • ✓Ownership sums to one hundred percent. Exactly, after rounding, with the residual landing somewhere deliberate rather than wherever the arithmetic dropped it.
  • ✓The fees charged to investors add up to the fee the fund paid. Side letters and differing rates included. If the pieces do not sum to the pool, one of the two is wrong.
  • ✓Every journal entry balances, and the trial balance foots. The ordinary one, checked the ordinary way, every day rather than at close.
  • ✓What a position is carried at agrees with what the ledger says it is carried at. Through every subscription, redemption and return of capital, so the two cannot drift apart between valuations.

When one of these fails it does not go into a log. It opens a break, on the same worklist as a cash break, with the fund, the period and the two figures that should have agreed.

Every morning, not at close

Timing is most of the value. An error found during the close is an error found under deadline, by the person who least wants to hear about it, at the point when the options are bad. The same error found on a Tuesday is an afternoon's work.

So the checks do not wait for a period to end, and they do not sample. Every fund is re-proved every day, including the ones nothing happened to, because "nothing happened" is itself a claim worth testing: a fund that quietly stopped receiving activity looks identical to a fund having a quiet month, right up until the quarter closes.

The part that is easy to skip: knowing whether a check actually ran

This is the half most systems get wrong, and it is worth being precise about, because it is the difference between a control and the appearance of one.

A check that never runs reports nothing. So does a check that runs and passes. On any dashboard they look identical, and the more conditional a check is, the more likely it is quietly doing nothing. A rule that only applies to funds with an incentive fee is silent across a book that has none, and silent is what passing looks like.

The distinction

A check that nothing has ever been run against is not a control. It is an untested assumption with a green light next to it.

So the morning run reports two things, not one. It reports what failed, and it reports what was never exercised, naming each check that no fund gave it anything to examine. Some of those are expected: a rule about converted funds has nothing to say about a book with no converted fund in it, and it says so rather than passing. Others are genuinely unproven, and they are listed as unproven rather than counted as passes. We would rather publish a shorter list of controls we can demonstrate than a longer one padded with rules nothing has tested.

Blank is a valid answer

The same principle shows up in the reports. Where a figure cannot be computed honestly, the cell is empty and says why, rather than carrying a number produced by an assumption nobody stated. A subtotal that would be meaningless is left out. A percentage that has lost its denominator does not guess one.

An empty cell looks like something failed, which is exactly why it is safe. A wrong cell looks like it worked, and that is the one that reaches an investor.

What to ask any vendor

  • ✓What is re-derived rather than stored? If every figure is computed once and read thereafter, nothing in the system can ever disagree with it, and no check can find it wrong.
  • ✓How often, and against how many funds? At close and on a sample is a different product from every day and on all of them.
  • ✓Can you show me a check that did not run? If the answer is that they all pass, ask how the system distinguishes passing from never having been tried.
  • ✓Where does a failure go? A log nobody reads and a worklist somebody owns are not the same control.

How we handle it

The morning run is not a feature we added, it is how the engine is developed: a rule is written as something that must always be true, and it runs against every fund from then on. It runs daily, after every release, and it reports what was never exercised alongside what failed. The most recent run before this note was published covered every fund on the platform and came back clean.
