What an opening balance has to carry

A trial balance tells you where a fund stands. It does not tell you enough to compute what happens next, because the engines that matter are stateful, and the state they need is not on any document you can request from a custodian.

Conversion · roughly 5 minutes

Standing still versus carrying on

A conversion is usually scoped as loading balances: cash, positions, receivables, payables, capital. That work is real and it is not the hard part. Balances describe a fund at rest. The question a new system has to answer on its first working day is the other one, which is what happens next, and several of the calculations that answer it depend on things that already happened.

The fee engine is the part that gets left behind

Positions and cash are physical. Somebody can pull a custody statement and check them, and if they are wrong it shows up immediately and loudly.

Fee-engine state is different in kind. A high-water mark is a memory of something that already happened. There is no counterparty holding a copy, no statement to reconcile against, and no external document that records it. It exists inside the system being switched off.

That makes it the most likely thing to be dropped and the least likely to be noticed. A fund converted without its marks reconciles perfectly on day one and stays perfect until the first incentive calculation, which will be wrong by an amount nobody can reconstruct, because the evidence needed to reconstruct it was in the system that has since been decommissioned.

Ask early

Whether the outgoing system can export marks, loss carryforwards and equalization positions per investor is worth establishing before a migration date is agreed, not after. If the answer is no, that is a constraint on the whole project, and it is cheaper to discover in a meeting than in a fee run.

Closed-end funds: the waterfall is path-dependent

For a drawdown fund the tiers are measured cumulatively across the life of the fund. What the next distribution pays depends entirely on what has already been paid: whether capital is back, whether the preferred return has been satisfied, whether the manager is still in catch-up.

A fund converted with a balance sheet and a commitment schedule has no position in that sequence at all. It cannot say which tier the next dollar belongs to, and the first distribution after the cut will be computed from the beginning of a waterfall that is already years in.

Uncalled commitment has the same shape. It is not commitment minus called: distributions made subject to recall are added back, and whether a given distribution was recallable is a term in the agreement rather than a property of the cash.

Two different things called history

Prior periods can come across in one of two ways, and they are routinely conflated in the same sentence.

 Reporting-onlyRebuilt
What arrivesThe results: balances, statements, capital accounts as struckThe underlying activity, transaction by transaction
What you can doDisplay it, print it, answer "show me last year"Re-run an allocation, restate a period, change an assumption
What it needsThe old system's outputThe old system's inputs, in usable form
Typical costPart of the conversionA project of its own, and often not possible

Reporting-only is an honest and usually correct answer. The failure is buying it while expecting the other one, and then discovering the difference during an audit. Pre-conversion periods should be marked as what they are, so that a snapshot cannot be mistaken for a period that would recompute if you asked it to.

The test that settles it

Before the old system is switched off, run the first post-conversion period on both and compare. Three specific comparisons are worth more than a reconciliation of totals:

  • ✓The incentive fee, per investor. Not the fund total. A fund total can be right while two investors are wrong in opposite directions, and that is exactly what a missing mark looks like.
  • ✓One realized sale. This tests that lots and the cost-flow convention came across, not just the position's market value.
  • ✓One distribution, on a closed-end fund. This tests that the fund knows where it stands in its own waterfall.

If that comparison cannot be run because the old system is already gone, the fund was not converted. It was re-keyed, and the first calculation that depends on history will be the one that finds out.

What to ask a vendor

  • ✓Is there a conversion date as a first-class concept, or does every fund have to start at inception?
  • ✓Where does a high-water mark go in? If there is no field for it, it is being reverse-engineered from a transaction history you do not have.
  • ✓Are pre-conversion periods marked, or do they present exactly like periods the system computed itself?
  • ✓What happens when an error is found in a pre-conversion period? There is a right answer, which is that it is corrected as a restatement with the original preserved, and a wrong one, which is that somebody edits the snapshot.
  • High-water marks and the partial redemption, the state most likely to be missing
  • What the platform does, including conversion-mode onboarding
  • Pricing, where converting an existing book is quoted separately
