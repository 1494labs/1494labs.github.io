Notes

Working notes on the parts of fund accounting that are hard to look up and harder to get right. Written for controllers, CFOs and administrators who have to make the numbers defensible, not for people shopping for software.

  • A wrong number that agrees with itself

    A break gets investigated because something disagrees. When the roll-forward foots and the statement ties, nothing raises its hand, and the figure can still be wrong. What it takes to catch that, and why it runs every morning rather than at close.

  • ABOR and IBOR, and why most systems keep them apart

    Two books, two clocks, and a reconciliation nobody wants to own. Where the split came from, what it costs, and four questions that separate the architectures faster than a feature matrix.

  • European vs American waterfalls, with the numbers worked

    The same two deals through both structures. Same fund, same total profit, and a six million dollar difference in what the manager is holding when the second deal disappoints.

  • Equalization, and what a mid-period subscriber actually owes

    An investor who buys after a drawdown rides the recovery without paying an incentive fee, because the loss being carried forward is somebody else's. Why one NAV per share cannot be right for two investors, and the two ways out.

  • High-water marks and the partial redemption

    Half the money leaves. The loss carried forward against the half that stayed has to fall by half too, and when it does not, nothing in the accounts disagrees. The error only ever runs one way.

  • What an opening balance has to carry

    A trial balance says where a fund stands, not enough to compute what happens next. A fund converted without its high-water marks reconciles perfectly for exactly one period.

  • Which columns can be added, and which cannot

    A weighted average and an average of averages differ by $300,000 a year on a $100m fund, and both look like a subtotal. Four classes of column, three behaviors, and why a blank cell is an answer.
