How fund accounting software is priced

Four axes are in common use, and they are not interchangeable. Which one a vendor picks tells you what they think they are selling, and it is worth knowing before the first call, because almost nobody in this segment publishes anything on the subject.

AxisWho uses it, and why it suits them
Per AUM basis points The fund administrator's model. Their cost really does rise with the size and activity of the book, because people do the work, and basis points track that. Software carries no such labor, so the same fee charges a growing customer more for no extra cost incurred, and penalizes the customer most worth keeping.
Per seat Suits software whose value scales with headcount. Fund accounting does not. A two-person administrator running forty funds would pay almost nothing, which is precisely backwards.
Flat platform fee Simple, and honest about the cost of having a customer at all. On its own it ignores how much book they keep, and treats five funds and fifty as the same thing.
Per fund, with a platform minimum A fund is a book: its own calendar, its own terms, its own close. The per-fund part tracks the work and the value, and the minimum covers the cost that actually binds a small vendor.

Published, or quoted

The market splits cleanly. The self-serve end, aimed at emerging managers with one or two funds, posts a monthly price. The entity-count end quotes, and usually adds an implementation fee running to five or six figures. There is very little in between, which is why a manager with a single fund tends to find that every option is an enterprise purchase.

Published starting prices from established vendors in this segment sit somewhere around $24,000 to $36,000 a year, before implementation. Treat those as a floor rather than a price: they are doing the work of disqualifying anyone who cannot write that cheque, which is a reasonable thing for a vendor to want and a useful thing for a buyer to recognise.

What actually drives a quote up

Fund count, because each fund is a separate book with its own close. This is the one most vendors price on, and the one most amenable to being published.

Structural complexity, because a master-feeder with series accounting, equalization and side pockets carries more mechanism than a single closed-end vehicle. This is where entity counting breaks down: a master and three feeders is four entities and one economic book, so counting them as four funds overstates the work, while per entity the structure is more intricate than a standalone fund, so counting understates it. It misses in both directions, which is why it usually ends up quoted.

Conversion of an existing book, because migrating a mid-life fund means carrying an opening position across rather than starting from inception: balances, capital accounts, and the fee-engine state that makes the next incentive calculation correct, which means high-water marks, loss carryforwards and equalization position. Rebuilding periods that were never captured, so that they compute rather than simply report, is a larger project again and worth asking about separately.

Questions worth asking a vendor

  • ✓What is the implementation fee, and what does it cover that the subscription does not?
  • ✓How do you count a master-feeder, and do share classes or series add to the number?
  • ✓Is converting our existing book priced separately, and what does it include?
  • ✓What happens to the price when we add a fund, and when we grow the ones we have?

The last one separates the axes quickly. Under basis points, growing costs you more for nothing extra. Under per fund, it does not.
