# Look-through consolidation for family offices

Add up the balance sheets of every entity a family owns and the total is wrong, sometimes by a factor of eight. Every layer of ownership counts the layer beneath it again. Consolidated reporting is the work of taking those duplicates out so each asset is counted once, at the share the family actually holds.

Family office · roughly 6 minutes

## Why the legal books overstate

Take a small chain. A trust owns 40% of a holding company. The holding company owns 60% of a family partnership. The partnership holds a $100m interest in an outside fund.

That $100m appears in the partnership's books. It appears again inside the holding company's investment in the partnership, and again inside the trust's investment in the holding company. Three sets of books, one fund interest. The trust's real share of it is 40% of 60%, which is 24%, or $24m.

Do that across four trusts, a holding company, a partnership, six asset vehicles and a co-investment vehicle, and you get the reference household below: $12.01bn of legal books and $1.50bn actually owned.

![A look-through consolidation showing legal books of $12.01bn against a consolidated $1.50bn, with a per-asset table giving legal owner, asset class, valuation basis, effective ownership, gross value and attributed value.](https://1494labs.com/assets/screens/look-through-consolidation.a6726248.jpg)

**Every row carries its effective ownership.** Forty percent of a $157.5m holding company is $63.0m. The percentage comes from the capital accounts, and the attributed value follows from it.

## Effective ownership comes from the capital accounts

In many family offices the ownership percentages live in a spreadsheet, keyed in by hand and updated when someone remembers to. It goes stale the first time a trust makes a distribution or one sibling buys part of another's interest.

Here, ownership weights are derived from capital holdings. When capital moves, the effective ownership moves with it, and so do the consolidation, the structure chart and the income allocations. They all read the same numbers.

## When one vehicle has several owners

Family structures are rarely clean trees. Two trusts can both own the holding company. A co-investment vehicle can be owned by the partnership and by an individual directly. Drawn as separate trees, a shared vehicle repeats on every branch and its value gets counted twice in any total built from the drawing.

The structure is kept as a graph instead: every vehicle appears once, with all of its owners converging on it. Allocations follow the same graph, so income on a shared vehicle is split among its owners in proportion to their capital and then carried up each owner's chain.

![An ownership structure drawn as a flow diagram: four family trusts converging on a holding company of $3.58bn, through a family partnership, out to six asset sleeves and a co-investment vehicle.](https://1494labs.com/assets/screens/structure-flow.49a61406.jpg)

**Value passing through each vehicle.** Band width is the value flowing through, so the holding company shows both its $157.5m in its own legal books and the $3.58bn it controls.

## What the consolidated view carries

Each asset row shows its legal owner, asset class, valuation basis, effective ownership, gross value and attributed value. Fund interests carry the as-of date of the manager's last statement, and company marks carry their valuation method. A fund still held at last quarter's statement shows that date on its row.

From there the net worth view groups value by category, by holder and by period, with the movement between periods. Exposure and concentration read from the same attributed values, so a family's concentration in one sector is measured on the family's attributed share of each holding.
